# Athena UPS battery and remote power recovery

**Research date:** 2026-09-26. **Decision:** restore trustworthy battery backup and make the workstation recoverable after both an exhausted UPS and a clean shutdown while UPS output remains live.

## What is established

Four independent investigations ([cdx](athena_ups_battery_and_remote_recovery__cdx.md), [grk](athena_ups_battery_and_remote_recovery__grk.md), [mus](athena_ups_battery_and_remote_recovery__mus.md), [gem](athena_ups_battery_and_remote_recovery__gem.md)) found the same local clues. I independently confirmed today that `powerpanel` **1.3.3** remains in dpkg's removed/config-files state, the motherboard identifies as **ASUS PRIME TRX40-PRO**, and `lsusb` shows no UPS. The prior `PPL-1.3.3-64bit.deb` install and `pwrstat -status` shell history tie `pwrstat` to CyberPower's PowerPanel, making **CyberPower a high-confidence manufacturer identification**. They do not identify the unit now supplying power. The USB management link and automatic UPS shutdown protection are currently absent.

The live network path reported by the researchers is Intel I211 Ethernet `enp67s0`, MAC `a8:5e:45:d0:74:53`, on the home LAN. This board has no BMC/IPMI. Its [manual](https://dlcdnets.asus.com/pub/ASUS/mb/SocketTRX4/PRIME_TRX40-PRO/E16115_PRIME_TRX40-PRO_UM_V2_WEB.pdf?model=PRIME+TRX40-PRO) documents `Restore On AC Power Loss`, `ErP Ready`, and `Power On By PCI-E` under Advanced → APM Configuration. Current firmware settings still need physical verification.

**Best model guess: CyberPower CP1500PFCLCD, with low confidence.** It is a plausible 1500 VA sine-wave workstation unit from the period when PowerPanel was installed, but `pwrstat` works with many CyberPower models. CP1500PFCLCDa, CP1350PFCLCD, and an AVR LCD unit remain possible. The guess is not a battery part number. The [NUT compatibility report](https://networkupstools.org/ddl/Cyber_Power_Systems/CP1500PFCLCD.html) records a 900 W CP1500PFCLCD, while [CyberPower's current page](https://www.cyberpowersystems.com/product/ups/pfc-sinewave/cp1500pfclcd/) lists 1000 W and *different battery cartridges by serial prefix*. This revision difference is why neither a guessed watt limit nor a guessed cartridge should drive a purchase.

## Resolve the battery decision at the unit

Read or photograph the rear/bottom model and serial label, front LCD, and outlet markings; CyberPower [locates its serial label there](https://www.cyberpowersystems.com/faqs/where-is-the-serial-number-located-on-the-unit/). Record the exact model/revision, serial prefix, manufacture date, rated **watts** as well as VA, battery alarm, and the actual load during representative CPU/GPU work. If the label is obscured, reconnect the data USB cable to a rear motherboard port and identify the UPS through NUT; treat physical model/serial markings as authoritative for replacement parts.

Until a self-test and runtime drill pass, treat the failed-battery alarm as **no dependable ride-through**. [CyberPower says lead-acid UPS batteries commonly last 3–5 years and recommends retiring the whole UPS after about eight years](https://www.cyberpowersystems.com/blog/the-lifecycle-of-a-ups-system/). A 2020-era unit is therefore a reasonable battery-replacement candidate *if* its electronics are healthy and its output rating has adequate headroom. Replace the whole unit if it is about eight years old or older, damaged, fails a test with a good battery, or cannot support the measured peak with roughly 30–35% headroom. A simulated-sine model that has proven incompatible with this active-PFC workstation is another reason to replace it. CPU/GPU nameplate ratings are a reason to **measure**, not an estimate of wall draw. Size any new UPS from measured watts and required shutdown time, choosing sine-wave output.

If the physical unit really is a CP1500PFCLCD, use CyberPower's model-and-serial battery lookup or its matching manual. The [current product page](https://www.cyberpowersystems.com/product/ups/pfc-sinewave/cp1500pfclcd/) specifies `RB1290X2` for CXX serials and `RB1280X2B` for CR9/CXF/CQC serials; an older revision may differ. Its battery is user-replaceable **but not hot-swappable**. Order the exact compatible cartridge, power down connected loads and follow that unit's replacement instructions, recharge for the specified interval (eight hours on the current model), self-test, then verify useful runtime under a controlled load. Recycle the removed lead-acid pack. Generic cells may save money but require exact electrical/mechanical fit and correct reuse of the harness; a guessed two-cell kit is not sufficient evidence.

## Make outage shutdown lead to a restart

Reconnect the UPS's USB data cable and use Debian's **Network UPS Tools** (`usbhid-ups`, local `upsd`/`upsmon` in standalone/primary mode). NUT [documents the complete shutdown design](https://networkupstools.org/docs/user-manual.chunked/ar01s06.html); its [CP1500PFCLCD device reports](https://networkupstools.org/ddl/Cyber_Power_Systems/CP1500PFCLCD.html) show status/runtime/load and `shutdown.return`, though the actual unit's command set must be checked. Use `upsc` to confirm model/status and `upscmd -l` to inspect supported commands. Run either NUT **or** CyberPower PowerPanel as the USB owner, not both. Current PowerPanel may be viable; the historical 1.3.3 install should not simply be restored.

Configure immediate on-battery/lost-communication alerts and an **early, measured** shutdown threshold, leaving enough battery to finish Debian's shutdown. Configure and verify NUT's power-down flag and late shutdown hook so the UPS drops its output *after* filesystems are quiescent and then restores it on utility return. [NUT warns](https://networkupstools.org/docs/man/usbhid-ups.html) that many CyberPower devices round delay values down in minute increments; its suggested minimums are `offdelay=60` and `ondelay=120`. Do not assume those values or `shutdown.return` behave identically on an unidentified revision—test them.

Set ASUS UEFI **Restore On AC Power Loss = Power On**. This boots after the PSU actually loses and regains AC. It does **not** by itself boot a host that NUT cleanly halted while UPS output stayed energized: standby power never disappeared. The verified NUT output-off/return sequence closes that race. `Last State` is less deterministic after an orderly halt. During a maintenance window, test a NUT forced-shutdown sequence and a controlled UPS-input outage/return; confirm that Debian halts before output drops and the board boots when output returns. Keep the workstation's power cable on a *battery-backed* outlet, not a surge-only one.

## Remote power when automatic recovery fails

| Method | What it covers | Limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ASUS AC restore plus verified NUT output cycle | Automatic restart after a long outage, including an orderly halt | Requires a real UPS-output off/on edge; offers no remote control or boot view. |
| Wired Wake-on-LAN | A soft-off host with PSU standby power present | Requires BIOS/NIC settings and a powered **LAN-side** sender; cannot diagnose failed POST. |
| PiKVM V4 Mini ATX control | Remote power-button/reset action and pre-OS video/keyboard | Costs roughly **$275 at US sellers** as listed by [PiKVM](https://pikvm.org/buy/) today; requires powered home network and WAN. |
| Smart plug cycling PC AC | Forces an AC-restore edge | Can cut a running filesystem, lacks boot view, and adds a high-current failure point; reserve for a documented last resort. |

For a free secondary path, set **ErP Ready = Disabled** and **Power On By PCI-E = Enabled** in UEFI, then persist magic-packet Wake-on-LAN on `enp67s0` through its NetworkManager wired profile. Confirm the NIC reports magic-packet wake capability and perform an actual S5 wake test. [Tailscale explains](https://tailscale.com/blog/wake-on-lan-tailscale-upsnap) that its Layer-3 network does not carry ordinary LAN WoL broadcasts, even through subnet routing: `apollo` in the cloud cannot wake this LAN NIC unaided. A powered router with a proven WoL function or a small tailnet-connected device **on the home LAN** must send the magic packet. Do not assume a particular router/app has this feature without testing it.

For a dependable manual power path, add a **PiKVM V4 Mini**. Its [kit includes an ATX control board](https://docs.pikvm.org/v4/) that can be installed between the case switch and motherboard header while preserving the physical button; connect HDMI and USB for UEFI/boot diagnosis. Power PiKVM from its own adapter on protected power, not from Athena's USB port. Put the ISP ONT/modem, router, necessary switch, and PiKVM on backed-up power (a separate small network UPS is an option if it materially improves runtime). [PiKVM documents Tailscale access](https://docs.pikvm.org/tailscale/); limit access to the tailnet and test it from outside the house. This provides an actual remote button if firmware auto-start, NUT, or WoL fails. Once the home network or ISP is down, remote control must wait for that path to return; no LAN-only design can guarantee access through an ISP outage.

## Recommended solution and UPS guess

**First identify the physical UPS and measure its load.** Replace its *serial-matched* battery if the unit is healthy and adequately sized; otherwise replace the entire UPS with a properly sized sine-wave model. Reconnect USB and deploy/test NUT's graceful-shutdown **and output-return** sequence. Set ASUS AC restore to **Power On**, enable PCI-E WoL with ErP disabled, and test a LAN-side wake. Because remote startup is an explicit requirement, add an independently powered **PiKVM V4 Mini on the home tailnet** for a real remote power button and boot console; back up the network equipment needed to reach it. Validate the whole chain under a controlled outage before relying on it while away. **My best UPS guess is CyberPower CP1500PFCLCD; manufacturer confidence is high, exact-model confidence is low until the chassis label or USB descriptor confirms it.**
